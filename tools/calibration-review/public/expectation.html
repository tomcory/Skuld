What you judge. Each unit is one practice (data sent to a recipient, or read on the device) and what E's first stage predicted for it: the purposes the processing serves and whether the data is needed for them. Judge the predictions against what the stage saw: the sentence it was given for the practice (grey box), and on the right the practice's traffic (the requests as captured, the data found in them highlighted), the screens of the episodes it occurred in and the documentation passages. The stage never sees the provider's privacy policy or store listing, so do not judge against them; if you know that the provider says otherwise, say so in the note. A screen may come with a description written by a model (purple box) and a screenshot (click to enlarge, Esc to close). The stage was given the descriptions and up to eight screenshots per episode; you see every screenshot taken, except of screens with a privacy or consent notice, which the stage never sees. The description helps you find your way; it is a model's reading, not evidence, and E may not cite it. The screenshot shows the screen as the user saw it. Screens in a grey block are one visit: the same screen whose content changed in place (text typed, a box ticked, a page loaded). They share one screenshot and one description, taken when the visit began; a screen marked before the change shows that earlier state, since no screenshot was taken of its own. Which model produced the unit is not shown.

Each question on its own. A flaw counts once, in the question it belongs to: an implausible purpose is a no on Plausible, not also on its node, need or steps. Judge the node, the need and the steps for the purpose as E states it, even where you find the purpose itself implausible. A purpose that repeats another is judged on its own; mention the repetition in the note. Answer unsure only where the context does not let you decide, and say why in the note.

  • Plausible: a plausible purpose for this flow, given the context. Ask yourself how much you would have to change E's wording before you would sign it.
    • Yes: nothing. Someone who knows the recipient and sees this flow would name this purpose as E states it.
    • Mostly: one phrase. It is the right purpose with one detail off, e.g. personalised advertising where the flow shows only ad delivery: strike "personalised" and you would sign it.
    • Partly: a rewrite. Something in it is right, but a substantial part is wrong or unsupported, or it explains only a minor part of the flow, e.g. "analytics and fraud prevention" for an ad network's ID sync, of which only a fraud check is at all plausible.
    • No: it does not fit what the recipient does, e.g. app performance analytics for an ad-tech ID sync.
    For mostly and partly, click one or more reasons (hover a reason for its meaning): Overstates (asserts a detail the context does not show), Too vague (too broad or generic to say what the recipient does), Bundles purposes (two or more purposes in one, e.g. analytics and advertising), Part of the flow (explains only part of what is sent, or to whom), Loose fit (the recipient could do this, but the flow gives little sign of it), or Other, which opens a field for a reason of your own (a few words). The reasons say why it is not a plain yes; the level says how far it is from one: an overstated detail is usually mostly, a loose fit usually partly.
  • Node: the DPV purpose node: exact, too broad, too narrow, wrong (name a better one if you can), for the purpose as E words it.
  • Beneficiary (user, provider, other) and role (functional: the feature the user uses; ancillary: anything else): right or wrong. Functional means the feature the user is using needs this processing; ads, consent dialogs, analytics, and fonts or resources loaded for an ad are ancillary even when they appear on the screen. Where the screens do not show which, decide by what the recipient does; unsure only if it serves both.
  • Cited parts show its outcome, for a purpose E holds supported: the parts it cites (tab Cited for on the right) show what the purpose achieves. Yes: they show that this purpose's outcome happens in this app, including what sets it apart (for personalised ads, something showing personalisation, not just that ads appear). Mostly: they show it, with a minor gap: one cited part is beside the point, or the distinguishing detail is suggested rather than shown. Partly: they show only the broader activity (ads appear), or only some of the cited parts show it. No: none of them shows it, e.g. only a general guideline or documentation passage.
  • Per need E stated (is the data, or the recipient, essential / quality-dependent / not needed for the purpose): is that plausible, and do E's steps follow from what they cite? Each step's citations are chips ( traffic, screen, passage): click one to read the cited text, hover for a preview. A need E holds not assessable is not asked; its reasoning can be shown.
    • Stated need plausible. Yes: it is the level you would choose. Mostly: defensible, though you would lean to the neighbouring level (a borderline case). Partly: clearly one level off (essential where quality-dependent fits, quality-dependent where not needed fits, or the reverse). No: two levels off (essential against not needed).
    • Steps follow from what they cite, whether or not you agree with the level (that is the question above). A step counts as supported if what it cites shows it, or if it is general technical knowledge you would accept without a source (e.g. that a WebView sends a User-Agent). Yes: every step the conclusion rests on is supported. Mostly: a minor step is unsupported, but the conclusion does not rest on it. Partly: at least one such step is an uncited claim about this app or recipient, or contradicts what it cites, but the other steps still carry the conclusion. No: without the unsupported steps nothing carries the conclusion.
  • Every answer short of a plain yes, mostly or partly, asks why: click one or more of the reasons that open below it (hover a reason for its meaning), or Other and say it in a few words. The level says how far the answer is from a yes; the reasons say why.
  • Missed: a purpose E should have predicted and did not; tick functional if it is the feature's own.
  • Values in code type (a data type, a purpose node) open their vocabulary when clicked: the tree with the value selected (marked "shown in the unit"), its description, the broader node, the nodes alongside it and the narrower ones, to judge whether it is exact, too broad or too narrow.
  • DPV node fields (a missed purpose, a better node): type part of a name for suggestions (↓ ↑ Enter), or click to browse the DPV purposes as a tree, filter it, read each node's description and select one.
  • The purposes are shown one at a time: the bar above them shows which one (its number) and how many of each one's questions are answered (a tick when all are); move with the numbers, the arrows, the button at the end of each purpose, or J (next) and K (previous). On the right, Cited for shows what E cites for the purpose you are on; the other tabs hold all the traffic, screens and passages the stage was given, colour-coded: ● traffic, ● screens, ● passages. Findings that repeat (the same field, datum and data type) are folded under the first: open them with +N more like this.
